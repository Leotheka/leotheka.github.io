Even If It Breaks, It's Worth Building

This blog was born from one of those moments of inspiration that hit me every now and then after watching an exceptional video, and the one behind it was Tom Scott with his most-viewed video (I have a habit of going to a creator's channel and checking what has worked best for them). That's how I found This Video Has X Views, whose hook (many think it's clickbait) is that it always shows the updated view count, no matter where or when you're watching it. And that video left me thinking about a few things.

Scott first explains how this is technically possible (using APIs) and then talks about the utopia APIs were in what came to be known as Web 2.0, an idyllic world of people building bots with Yahoo, Twitter and Google Maps, and how that tech boom later became more and more restricted (the occupational hazards of governance?). What was once a free playground for hobbyists is now a formal environment of communication protocols: we have more technology, but also more rules. That has helped us connect with each other, but that interconnection comes at a price. If YouTube shuts down its API tomorrow or changes the protocol, the video's "magic" could break. That it still works six years later is poetic... How much longer will that code survive? Years? Centuries?

It reminds me of Bradbury's There Will Come Soft Rains, where technology keeps running even when we're no longer around, like a remnant of what we were.

But anyway, I won't get too existential. What actually inspired me was that, at the end, he reflects that we should build things anyway, even if they don't last forever, even if they break... because in the end, if it helps one person, it was already worth it.

That's what I find wonderful about human beings: that gift (or maybe nature?) for service that we have. I think no one captures it better than Borges in his poem The Just (maybe Casciari does it a little better with his cover).

This is an attempt to be one of those just. A space to share reflections that maybe no one will read, but that maybe someone will, and that hopefully invite you to think and to pause... how important it is to pause these days.

I say this thinking of something I heard from Terence Tao. He imagines science as a trail, like when you set out looking for a waterfall or a mountain peak: you're very likely to get lost, but getting lost has a lot of value. What AI is doing is taking us straight there, by helicopter. We get where we wanted, yes, but the path is lost, and with it that slowness that is so useful.

Welcome to the Leotheka.
